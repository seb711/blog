Everything started the day before yesterday when i was preparing some extra material for an exercise session held in university.
The syllabus of this course is mostly about how to write efficient specialized data structures; students should learn how to trade-off different design decisions based on the workloads the datastructures (like B-Trees, Tries, ...) are used in.
As the semester is not that old yet, we are still at the beginning and started with Hash-Tables and Hash-Functions.
Hash-Functions is a black art for itself and I do not really want to go into depth here as this is not too relevant for this blogpost.
So here I was thursday afternoon in my office talking about the Hash-Function-Slides with my colleague: I have implemented five different Hash-Functions and wanted to see how they perform in terms of performance (cycles per hash) and in terms of robustness (avalanche-effect).
The candidates were MurmurHash, xxHash64, FNSV, CRC32 (with hardware support) and SHA-256 (as a crpytographic hash function).
First i started with the performance benchmarks, herefore i generated a list of 200K keys and then I hashed all of them multiple times (ITER=1M).
After a warm-up phase the benchmark starts and with performance measuring counters (PMC) the program counts user cycles and kernel cycles.
The benchmark was executed on my Framework 13 with AMD Ryzen 5 7640U (up to 4.9GHz) running Arch Linux (kernel version 6.18.9.arch1-2):

| hash_fn | time | cycle | kcycle | IPC | GHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MurmurHash3 | 0.16 | 7.12 | 0.11 | 4.07 | 4.60 |
| xxHash64 | 0.24 | 11.18 | 0.17 | 3.58 | 4.73 |
| FNV-1a | 0.28 | 12.92 | 0.19 | 4.18 | 4.75 |
| CRC32c | 0.24 | 11.18 | 0.17 | 1.97 | 4.84 |
| SHA-256 | 18.05 | 839 | 12.86 | 3.19 | 4.76 |

## Act I: Something Is Wrong Here

First time I ran this benchmark I was not too concerned with the numbers as they proofed what we wanted to proof: crpytographic hash-functions are pretty slow, and the more never ones (MurmurHash, xxHash64) are relatively comparable in terms of performance.
When showing this to another PhD student and former exercise session tutor he said immediately: "Wow why are the Kernel Cycles (kcycles) so high?".
This was particulary interesting, because it was ~1% of overall runtime.
So I immediately started looking into that; the only thing that could cause these kernel cycles were some interrupt handlers that were running.
Because I am also researching in interrupt handling I tried to make sense of it.
My linux sends hw timer interrupts with 1000Hz (every 1ms): I knew that the interrupt handler probably takes around ~1us from entry to finish.
If my benchmark runs 1 seconds, it would receive 1 thousand interrupts each costing around ~1us resulting in around 1ms overall.
So around 0.1% of the total runtime.
This is by the factor of 10 off my observed numbers.
Because of other obligations I had to stop my investigation there.

## Act II: The Holiday That Wasn't

The next day was public holiday and I thought to just spend the day in the sun, but when this problem entered my mind again i just had to look further into it.
I ran this benchmark on my workstation and the result surprised me:

Workstation with Ubuntu Linux with an AMD Ryzen 9 9900X (up to 5.5GHz) (kernel version: 6.17.0-23-generic):

| hash_fn | time | cycle | kcycle | IPC | GHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MurmurHash3 | 0.17 | 8.09 | 0.01 | 4.21 | 4.76 |
| xxHash64 | 0.21 | 11.02 | 0.02 | 4.08 | 5.48 |
| FNV-1a | 0.27 | 14.61 | 0.02 | 4.04 | 5.51 |
| CRC32c | 0.19 | 10.02 | 0.01 | 2.70 | 5.49 |
| SHA-256 | 15.91 | 855 | 1.06 | 3.60 | 5.50 |

The workstation actually provided the kernel cycles overhead of my napkin math calculation.
So something was off with the my laptop setup, therefore i checked the amount of interrupts with perf stat only running the MurmurHash3 iteration, but they were similar.

```
            irq_vectors:local_timer_entry/s
Framework   1001
Workstation 1031
```

Ok this even weirder they roughly do the same interrupts (both have preemption configured to 1000Hz).
Both run on the same kernel version so the kernel cannot be the issue, as they should execute the same logic.

## Act III: The Flame Graphs Don't Lie

My last chance to get to the core of the problem was seeing where most of the time was spent inside of the interrupts.
So i ran the benchmark with:

```bash
perf record -e k:cycles --count=100000 --call-graph dwarf ./hash-bench
```

This resulted in the following two flamegraphs (left is Framework, right is Workstation):

| [![]({{ "/assets/images/htep/flame.svg" | relative_url }})]({{ "/assets/images/htep/flame.svg" | relative_url }}) | [![]({{ "/assets/images/htep/flame_office.svg" | relative_url }})]({{ "/assets/images/htep/flame_office.svg" | relative_url }}) |
|:---:|:---:|
| Framework | Workstation |

What is directly jumping in the eye is the read_hpet()-call that is only visible in the Framework flamegraph.
HPET stands for High Precision Event Timer and "is a hardware timer available in modern x86-compatible personal computers".
I did not know that this is the default but also looked what the Workstation is doing and couldn't find any `read_hpet()`-frames, but `read_tsc()` frames.
So what was going on here? I never have configured something on my Framework Linux setup to not use the TSC but the HPET.

## Act IV: The Kernel Knew All Along

So i checked out the kernel logs to check if the kernel has something to say about that; fortunately yes:


Ok so in the end it seems to be a skillissue on my side and I have somehow misconfigured the BIOS on my machine.

## Act V: Why Does HPET Cost So Much?

Hugh, great! But why does it cost more to read from HPET than TSC.
As it seems this is also a problem in audio and gaming, therefore I refer to this article: [OSDev HPET](https://wiki.osdev.org/HPET)
The HPET timer has a access latency of an additional 500-1000ns. So this is almost doubling the cost.
Where the additional factor 5 is coming from is not 100% clear to me but this is also probably related to the access path of HPET (the interrupt takes approx. 10us on avg.).

## Epilogue: Lessons Learned

So what did we learn: SHA-256 is not a good hash function for hash-tables and maybe check the kernel logs first if you see more kernel-cycles than expected in your benchmark.
